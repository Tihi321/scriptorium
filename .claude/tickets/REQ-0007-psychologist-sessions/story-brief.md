## Premise

Dr Ruth Penhallow, 58, is a psychologist with a small private practice above Okafor's bakery on Sheep Street in Wenlow, an English market town on a canal. She has a kettle, a fern that refuses to die or to thrive, and a great deal of common sense. Over one year, from September to the following June, five patients with slightly loony but entirely ordinary problems climb her stairs three times each: a man at war with his neighbour over a hedge, a woman who plans her wedding by spreadsheet, a man who apologises to furniture, a widow who hides her tango lessons from her helpful children, and a man who has read every self-help book and diagnoses everyone he meets. Each of the fifteen stories is one session and a complete comic short story, with its own problem, turn and ending, and a small truth that the story lets happen rather than says. Around the sessions, Ruth's own life moves on: her husband Alan builds a canal boat in the back garden that everyone agrees will never float, her grown daughter Jess comes home and then leaves again, her mother Edna gives the blunt version of everything from her care home, and Mr Okafor's ovens downstairs set the rhythm of every hour. The common sense runs both ways. Ruth's one flaw is that she cannot stop being right at home, and across the year her patients, without meaning to, teach her to hold the rope and say nothing.

## Plot

A linked collection of fifteen sessions, each about 3,000 words and readable alone. The order is interleaved: stories 1-5 are the first visits of Gordon, Priya, Kevin, Marjorie and Owen, in that order; stories 6-10 their second visits in the same order; stories 11-15 their third. Stories 1-5 fall in September and October, 6-10 in November to January, 11-15 in April to June. Each story is told mostly in the consulting room, but may follow a patient or Ruth out of it for the turn or the ending. Each story also carries one or two beats of Ruth's own life, so the book is never only patients and psychologist.

Ruth's own thread: she gives good advice all day and then cannot stop giving it at home. She has said "it will never float" about Alan's boat at least once a week for six years, and when Jess comes home she makes her a plan. Her patients' troubles quietly hold up a mirror (stories 4, 10, 11), her mother says the true thing (story 14), and in story 15 she stands on the towpath, admits she does not know, and says nothing at all.

1. **The Hedge Dossier** (Gordon Pike, first visit; September).
   - Problem: Gordon's leylandii hedge on Orchard Close has been at war with his neighbour Desmond Fry for four years. Letters, photographs, a laser level, a cherry-picker Desmond hired to photograph the top. Gordon's GP sent him because of his blood pressure; Gordon has come to prove he is in the right.
   - What happens: Gordon arrives with a ring binder and a shoebox scale model of the hedge, complete with cotton-wool clouds. He takes Ruth through four years of evidence, measured at dawn each Sunday. Ruth listens, makes tea, and asks him how he would spend a Saturday if the hedge were settled tomorrow. He cannot answer. Since he sold his plant-hire firm the feud has been his whole calendar.
   - Turn: Ruth suggests one Sunday without the tape measure, as an experiment, not a surrender.
   - Ending: the next Sunday Gordon stays in bed until seven, then looks out of the window and sees Desmond in his dressing gown in the garden, holding a tape measure, waiting for him, looking lost. Gordon does not go out. He feels oddly sorry for Desmond, which is new.
   - Moral: you can't win a fight you won't let end.
   - Side characters: Mr Okafor's 10:15 batch fills the room with heat and the smell of bread at the exact moment Gordon reaches the worst year of the feud; the ovens are introduced as the practice's clock. At home that evening Alan is sawing in the garden under a work lamp, the half-built canal boat on trestles beside the shed, and Ruth says, kindly and for the three-hundredth time, that it will never float. Alan says that is what they said about the Ark. Ruth's habit of having the last word is set up here, lightly.

2. **Fourteen Tabs** (Priya Nair, first visit; September).
   - Problem: Priya chose Ruth from a spreadsheet of nine local therapists, weighted by distance, reviews and parking. Now she must choose a wedding venue and the spreadsheet has fourteen tabs and no winner. Her fiancé Ben Croft would marry her in a bus shelter. Her mother Lakshmi Nair wants a hotel with chandeliers.
   - What happens: Priya opens her laptop on Ruth's desk and asks Ruth to score the venues with her. Ruth gamely tries. Each new column (acoustics, "vibe", distance from the nearest motorway services) makes it worse.
   - Turn: the old wiring of the building trips when Mr Okafor's ovens and Ruth's kettle run together; the lights go out and Priya's laptop, on two per cent, dies. In the grey light from the window Ruth asks her which venue she smiled about while she was typing it in. Priya, without the screen, says the cricket pavilion at once, and then blushes.
   - Ending: Priya rings Ben from the stairs. He says, "The pavilion? Brilliant," and she realises he has been waiting three months to be allowed to say it. She leaves the laptop charger behind; Ruth keeps it in a drawer until story 7.
   - Moral: not every choice needs a column.
   - Side characters: Mr Okafor comes up with a torch and a tray of iced buns by way of apology, and resets the fuse, as he has done for eleven years. That evening Edna rings Ruth from Larkhill House to complain about the custard and, told nothing confidential, only that "a young woman can't choose", says, "Tell her to toss a coin and see which way she hopes it lands." Edna is introduced as refusing every activity at the home: "I'm not doing chair yoga, Ruth. I'm waiting."

3. **The Sorry Tally** (Kevin Bright, first visit; October).
   - Problem: Kevin, 45, who works at the building society on the High Street, apologises to everyone, all the time, including the door, the chair and, on arrival, the fern. His sister Dawn Bright booked the appointment for him.
   - What happens: Ruth keeps a tally with her pencil. Forty-seven apologies in twenty minutes, including one to the tally. She proposes an experiment: no "sorry" until the end of the session. Kevin manages with increasingly baroque substitutes ("I regret the inconvenience of my general existence"), which are funnier and worse.
   - Turn: leaving, he finds Mr Okafor's flour delivery van parked across his car. The driver is eating a sausage roll and is in no hurry. Ruth watches from the window as Kevin walks up to the van three times and back twice.
   - Ending: on the third attempt he says, "Excuse me, you're blocking my car," and the driver says, "Right you are, mate," and moves. Nothing else happens. Kevin comes back up the stairs to tell Ruth, says "Sorry for coming back," hears himself, and laughs out loud for the first time in the session.
   - Moral: sorry is not a tax.
   - Side characters: that night Jess, 29, is on the doorstep of the house on Mill Lane with four bin bags and a rubber plant, having lost her London job and her relationship in the same week. Her first words are "Sorry, Mum, sorry, I should have rung." Ruth hears Kevin in her daughter. By bedtime she has started writing Jess a plan for getting back on her feet, which nobody asked for.

4. **Busy on Tuesday** (Marjorie Lyle, first visit; October).
   - Problem: Marjorie, 72, widowed, has two grown children who "help": her daughter Fiona Marsh rings every morning at nine and has a spare key; her son Graham Lyle has fitted a doorbell camera "for peace of mind". To get two evenings a week to herself, Marjorie has invented a book club, and, so that it sounds real, a friend in it called Pam. In fact she goes to a tango class at the Corn Exchange, taught by Clive Dunmore. She has come because she is afraid the lying means she is going dotty, which is what Fiona already suspects.
   - What happens: Marjorie's phone rings five times in the session (Fiona, Fiona, Graham, Fiona, a man about her boiler that Graham arranged). Ruth asks her what she would do with one week that nobody managed. Marjorie stands up and shows her: an ocho, a turn, a dramatic stop.
   - Turn: the floor of the old building bounces; downstairs Mr Okafor knocks on the ceiling with a broom handle, three polite taps. Marjorie, mid-step, laughs until she has to sit down. She is not dotty. She is busy.
   - Ending: Marjorie switches the phone off in front of Ruth, the first time in a year. When Fiona rings that evening Marjorie says "I'm busy on Tuesday," and does not explain, and finds it is the first true thing she has said about Tuesdays. Pam, for now, lives on.
   - Moral: you may have a life your children don't manage.
   - Side characters: Mr Okafor, at the foot of the stairs afterwards, tells Ruth he did not mind the dancing; it is good for bread to have a bit of life upstairs. At home Ruth hands Jess the plan, typed and laminated: graduate schemes, a gym timetable, a list of contacts. Jess thanks her far too much and puts it under a fridge magnet. Ruth, washing up, hears Marjorie's word "helping" and winces, and does not quite know why.

5. **Page Thirty-One** (Owen Price, first visit; October).
   - Problem: Owen, 39, a regional manager for a carpet warehouse, has read every self-help book and arrives early to diagnose the room: the fern is codependent (it leans towards the window), Ruth's tea is a comfort ritual, Mr Okafor's oven timer is Pavlovian. He wants Ruth to recommend the next book. Underneath: since his mother died two years ago, he and his father Roy Price, a retired bus driver, only exchange texts, mostly "Ok". Owen has a forty-page plan, from six frameworks, for repairing the relationship. He has not rung his father once.
   - What happens: Owen explains the plan, the stages, the scripts, the contingencies. Ruth asks which page tells him to ring. He says page thirty-one, after the groundwork.
   - Turn: Ruth pushes her desk phone across to him and goes to water the fern.
   - Ending: Roy answers, "What's wrong?" Nothing is wrong. They talk about the football for four minutes and the price of a pint for one. Roy says, "Ring again Sunday," and puts the phone down. Owen sits with the receiver in his hand. He leaves without asking for a book.
   - Moral: knowing is not doing.
   - Side characters: Alan picks Ruth up in the car, sawdust on his jumper. Owen, passing on the stairs, tells Alan that a man building a boat in a garden is preparing for a flood; Alan says he is preparing for a canal. At home Jess is still in pyjamas at four in the afternoon, and the laminated plan has a coffee ring on it.

6. **What Desmond Wants** (Gordon Pike, second visit; November).
   - Problem: Gordon has kept his Sundays free, and feels better, but now Desmond has put up a trellis. Gordon is sure it is a new attack and is ready to go back to war. He has never once asked Desmond what he actually wants; he assumes it is light, or spite.
   - What happens: Ruth asks Gordon to sit in the other chair and be Desmond. Gordon turns his flat cap backwards for the part and, to his own surprise, cannot do Desmond's voice for long without going quiet. He realises he knows nothing about the man except his hedge opinions.
   - Turn: Ruth asks what he would lose by knocking on the door and asking. Gordon says his dignity. Ruth says he could bring it back afterwards.
   - Ending: Gordon knocks. Desmond, flustered, shows him the sundial in the middle of his lawn: Maureen's sundial. His late wife Maureen Fry set it by the noon sun, and since the hedge grew it has been in shade, and he has not known what time it is, in any sense, for three years. The trellis is for her roses. On Saturday Gordon gets his long ladder and cuts a square window in the top of the leylandii, exactly where the noon sun comes through. The sundial tells the time again. The hedge stays.
   - Moral: ask what the other side wants.
   - Side characters: Ruth visits Edna at Larkhill House and, still bound by confidentiality, speaks only in generalities about "a man and his neighbour". Edna says, "Men are idiots. Tell him to knock on the door." Ruth says she more or less did. Edna is still refusing every activity, but has started reading the activities board in order to refuse each one by name, which Ruth takes as progress.

7. **Good Enough Cake** (Priya Nair, second visit; November).
   - Problem: venue settled, Priya now cannot choose a wedding cake. She has tasted forty samples from nine bakers and scored them on a ten-point scale with half points; three are tied at 8.5 and she cannot bear to choose a cake that is not the best.
   - What happens: Priya brings the three finalist samples in a cool box. Ruth eats them, which is the best part of her week. Priya admits Ben has started his own spreadsheet, to show he cares, and it is worse than hers, and she cried at it. Ruth hands her back the laptop charger from story 2.
   - Turn: Mr Okafor, delivering Ruth's morning loaf, sees the cool box and says, mildly, that one of the 8.5s is his. He tells Priya that a cake is good enough when the people eating it are happy, and that he has never made a perfect one in thirty-one years.
   - Ending: Priya picks his lemon sponge in under a minute. She deletes the cake tab on the spot and is surprised that the world stays where it is. Ben sends a text: a single thumbs-up and a cake emoji. Priya reads it three times and decides it is a perfect text.
   - Moral: good enough is a decision.
   - Side characters: Mr Okafor mentions his back is bad and he has nobody to lift the flour sacks; he has worked six days a week for thirty-one years and will not hear of stopping. Jess, it turns out, has started on the bakery counter that week, her own idea and not on Ruth's plan. Ruth finds out from Mr Okafor and is put out to have been the last to know.

8. **The Turkey That Didn't Fit** (Kevin Bright, second visit; December).
   - Problem: Kevin has nearly stopped saying sorry and now says yes instead, to everything. He has agreed to host Christmas for Dawn's family, twelve people, in his one-bedroom flat; to cover three colleagues' shifts; to sing in a choir although he cannot sing; and to sit on the Christmas lights committee. He has bought a nine-kilo turkey that does not fit his oven.
   - What happens: Kevin shows Ruth his diary, which is full to Twelfth Night. Ruth asks him, to practise, to take the fern home over Christmas and water it daily. He agrees at once. She asks him to try again and say no. It takes him four goes and a glass of water.
   - Turn: on his way out he asks Mr Okafor, downstairs, whether he could roast the turkey in the bread oven. Mr Okafor says, "No, Kevin. That oven is for bread, and Christmas morning is for my sleep. But I will sell you a smaller bird's worth of stuffing, and lend you my big tin." Kevin sees what a kind no looks like, from the other side.
   - Ending: Kevin rings Dawn from the bottom of the stairs: "I can't host. But I'll bring the pudding and do all the washing-up." There is a pause, and then Dawn says, enormously relieved, that she only offered his flat because she thought he wanted to be asked. The turkey goes back to the butcher.
   - Moral: no can be kind.
   - Side characters: Ruth remembers that the week before she asked Alan to stop work on the boat until after Christmas and help with the decorations, and Alan said, "No, love. But I'll do the lights the minute the rudder's on." She was cross, and the lights went up anyway, and they were lovely. Alan is changing: he has begun to work on the boat in daylight, openly, instead of after Ruth has gone to bed.

9. **Pam Does Not Exist** (Marjorie Lyle, second visit; January).
   - Problem: the book club has grown. Fiona wants to join it. Graham's doorbell camera has recorded his mother leaving at seven in the evening in heels, and the two of them now suspect either a gentleman or a scam. Marjorie has had to read the club's invented book of the month, invent Pam's opinions of it, and send Pam a birthday card. She is exhausted.
   - What happens: Marjorie lists the lies on Ruth's notepad. They fill both sides. She admits that Clive has asked her to be his partner at the spring tea dance, and she has said yes, and has no idea how to explain where she will be.
   - Turn: Fiona, in town for a dentist appointment, comes into Okafor's downstairs for a loaf just as Marjorie is due to leave. Marjorie hides on Ruth's landing for eleven minutes behind the coat stand, which is the turn: she hears herself whispering "this is ridiculous" and agrees with it.
   - Ending: that weekend Marjorie tells her children there is no book club. Pam does not exist. She goes dancing. Fiona is hurt that she was lied to and relieved that it is not a scam. Graham asks, after a long silence, whether Clive is "a nice man". Marjorie says he is a very good dancer, which is not quite an answer, and the doorbell camera is quietly switched off.
   - Moral: secrets cost more than the truth.
   - Side characters: Jess, now on the early shift at the bakery, serves Fiona her loaf and chats to her about sourdough without knowing who she is; from upstairs Ruth hears her daughter laugh for the first time since October. Mr Okafor has begun letting Jess shape the rolls.

10. **A Liminal Phase** (Owen Price, second visit; January).
    - Problem: Owen now rings his father every Sunday, but has turned the books on everyone else. There are attachment-style charts on the fridge. He has given his colleagues personality types. His partner Hannah Lowe, a vet, has gone to stay at her sister's "for a week", which has been nine days.
    - What happens: on the stairs Owen tells Jess, who is bringing up Ruth's loaf, that she is "in a liminal phase". Jess says she is just a person who is tired and goes back down. In the session Owen diagnoses Hannah in technical words. Ruth asks him to describe Hannah without a single term from a book. He cannot, at first. Then he tells her Hannah sings to the dogs at the surgery when they come round from the anaesthetic, badly, always the same song.
   - Turn: Owen, stung, turns the tables and tells Ruth that she does it too: she treats her own family as a project. He means it as a clever point. Ruth laughs, and then does not, because she has a laminated plan on her fridge at home.
   - Ending: Owen goes home and takes the charts off the fridge. He buys Hannah flowers with no plan attached and drives to her sister's. She comes home that night. Ruth, at home, takes the laminated plan down from the fridge and puts it in the recycling without a word to Jess, who notices and says nothing either.
    - Moral: people are not projects.
    - Side characters: Jess is changed from the girl on the doorstep: up at four, flour in her hair, cheerful and blunt with Owen. Ruth's flaw is named aloud for the first time, by the least likely person.

11. **Being Right on Orchard Close** (Gordon Pike, third visit; April).
    - Problem: Gordon and Desmond now have tea on Thursdays. But a surveyor's letter about Gordon's deeds has shown that the boundary fence stands thirty centimetres onto Gordon's land, and the strip in question is Maureen's rose bed. Gordon is legally right. He wants Ruth to tell him he is right.
    - What happens: Ruth agrees that he is right. Gordon waits for more and nothing comes. He talks through what being right would get him: a strip of soil, a new fence, a neighbour he would no longer have tea with, and Thursdays alone again.
    - Turn: Gordon realises he has been right about the hedge for four years and lonely for all of them.
    - Ending: Gordon puts the surveyor's letter in a drawer and never mentions it to Desmond. Leaving, he says he and Desmond have a job on in June: he has answered a card in the newsagent's window from a man on Mill Lane who needs a canal boat lifted out of his back garden on a low-loader. Ruth realises it is her garden and her husband. She cannot say a word, and does not. On the drive home she counts how many times she has been right about the boat, and finds the number does not make her feel any better.
    - Moral: being right is lonely.
    - Side characters: this is the first story where a patient thread touches Ruth's life. Alan has put the card in the newsagent's window himself without telling her: he is asking for help in public, and planning the launch for a Saturday in June. Ruth says nothing about it at dinner, which costs her a great deal.

12. **Cry at 14:20** (Priya Nair, third visit; May).
    - Problem: the wedding is in nine days. Priya has a schedule to the minute: arrive 13:40, vows 14:05, "emotional moment" during the speeches at 14:20. She has come because she does not feel anything yet, and she is afraid she never will, and that this means something terrible.
    - What happens: Priya walks Ruth through the schedule. Ruth asks when, in her life, she has cried on purpose at the time she meant to. Priya laughs and admits she cried at her graduation in the car park, two hours late, because a seagull took her sandwich. Lakshmi rings to ask about napkins and Priya, to her own surprise, says "good enough" and hangs up.
    - Turn: Priya deletes the column called "feelings" from the schedule. She keeps the rest. Ruth does not tell her to delete anything.
    - Ending: a postcard comes to the practice three weeks later, with a photograph of the cricket pavilion. Priya writes that she did not cry during the speeches at all. She cried at 11:52 that morning, buttering toast in her mother's kitchen, when Ben texted her a picture of his shoes. Nothing was scheduled at 11:52.
    - Moral: you can't schedule a feeling.
    - Side characters: Mr Okafor's lemon sponge is the wedding cake (story 7), and Jess baked the bread for the reception, her first big order. Mr Okafor offers Jess the early bake for good; his back is better now that he does not lift the sacks. Jess has started looking at flats and, when Ruth begins to say something about deposits, Ruth stops in the middle of the sentence.

13. **Eleven Seconds** (Kevin Bright, third visit; May).
    - Problem: Kevin has been nominated for the Wenlow Good Neighbour award by Dawn and half the High Street, for years of quiet help nobody ever thanked him for because he would never let them. He wants to decline. He has written a four-page speech explaining why other people deserve it more.
    - What happens: Kevin reads the speech aloud. It is generous, funny and entirely about other people. While he talks he notices that Ruth's sash window, which has been stuck all year, is stuck, and fixes it with a butter knife and a candle stub in two minutes. The bakery smell comes in properly for the first time in months.
    - Turn: Ruth thanks him. He says "it was nothing", "anyone would", "honestly it's the window's fault". She asks him to try once more, and this time to say only "thank you" and then nothing. The silence afterwards lasts eleven seconds and is, he says, the longest of his life.
    - Ending: at the award evening at the Corn Exchange Kevin walks up, takes the certificate, says "Thank you," and sits down. It is the shortest speech in the history of the award and gets the loudest applause. Dawn cries. Kevin lets her.
    - Moral: let a thank-you land.
    - Side characters: Mr Okafor and Jess do the catering together, and it is Jess who tells Ruth about the speech the next morning, because Ruth could not go (she has a patient's confidence to keep). Alan has finished painting the hull and has covered the name with a sheet; he will not tell Ruth what it says. Ruth thanks Alan that night for the new shelf he put up months ago, and makes herself stop there, and it lands.

14. **Dancing at Larkhill House** (Marjorie Lyle, third visit; June).
    - Problem: Marjorie and Clive are to dance at the Sunday tea dance at Larkhill House, the care home. Her children know about the tango now but she has not invited them. She is afraid they will fuss, or film it, or laugh, or worst of all be kind about it.
    - What happens: Marjorie tells Ruth all the reasons not to invite them. Ruth, who knows that she herself will be at Larkhill House that Sunday visiting her mother, says nothing about that and asks what Marjorie would want Fiona and Graham to have seen, if they had never seen it. Marjorie says: me, good at something.
    - Turn: Marjorie phones Fiona from Ruth's landing and invites them both. She tells Fiona she may film it, and may not help.
    - Ending: at Larkhill House on Sunday, Ruth is with Edna in the lounge when the dancers come in. She and Marjorie pretend, very carefully, never to have met. Marjorie and Clive dance; Fiona films and cries; Graham claps far too early and then far too long. Clive asks Edna to dance, and Edna, who has refused every activity for a year, dances seated, holding his hands, and is very good. Afterwards, with tea, Edna says to Ruth, without looking at her, "I'm proud of you, you know. Don't make a fuss." Ruth does not make a fuss.
    - Moral: let them be proud of you.
    - Side characters: this is the second story where a patient thread touches Ruth's life, and the one where Edna changes most: from "I'm waiting" to dancing, and to saying the thing Ruth has waited fifty-eight years to hear. The day before, Jess moved into her own flat over the hardware shop on Sheep Street; Ruth carried one box up the stairs and gave no advice at all, and Jess hugged her at the door.

15. **Told You So** (Owen Price, third visit; June).
    - Problem: Hannah is pregnant. Owen has read thirty-one books on parenting and needs to know, for certain, whether he will be a good father. He wants Ruth to tell him. It is a Saturday morning session; Ruth has fitted him in before the launch.
    - What happens: Owen lists everything he has learnt and everything that could go wrong. He asks Ruth, directly, what she thinks. Ruth tells him the truth: she does not know. Then, against her habit, she tells him a small true thing about herself: that this afternoon something she has been sure of for six years will be tested, and she does not know how it will go, and she has decided to go and watch.
    - Turn: Owen, for once, does not analyse what she has said. He asks if he can just sit for a minute. They sit. The ovens tick downstairs.
    - Ending: that afternoon Gordon's low-loader, with Desmond riding in the cab, brings the boat down Mill Lane to the slipway on the Wenlow Arm. A small crowd gathers. Owen and Hannah, out on their Saturday walk along the towpath, stop at the edge of it; Owen says nothing clever. Gordon gives Ruth an enormous wink and says, "We've never met, have we." The boat goes in, settles, lists a little, and floats. Alan pulls the sheet off the name: Told You So. He says it is for both of them, and does not say which way round. Ruth holds the rope and says nothing at all.
    - Moral: sit with not knowing.
    - Side characters: everyone has changed. Alan has finished, and no longer needs Ruth to believe in the boat. Jess brings two loaves from Okafor's that she baked at four that morning, and walks down from her own flat. Mr Okafor has taken his first Saturday afternoon off in thirty-one years, because Jess is minding the shop. Edna comes in a wheelchair taxi, although she said she would be dead before that thing floated, and has a hip flask. Ruth's flaw is settled without a speech: she was wrong, and is glad.

## Characters

### Ruth Penhallow
Role: the psychologist; the point-of-view character in every story
58. Dr Ruth Penhallow, clinical psychologist in private practice above Okafor's bakery on Sheep Street for eleven years. Tall, grey hair cut short, reading glasses on a cord, cardigans with pockets full of tissues and pencils. Born in Cornwall, still says "my lover" to the postman by accident. Voice: dry, warm, few words, questions rather than answers; patients call her Dr Penhallow. Keeps confidentiality absolutely, even when it is comic to do so. Her flaw: she cannot stop being right at home. She has told Alan his boat will never float for six years, and she manages Jess the way she would never let a patient be managed. She learns this across the book, quietly.

### Alan Penhallow
Role: Ruth's husband
62. Retired geography teacher, thirty-four years at the town secondary school. Big, slow, bald, beard, sawdust on every jumper. Voice: gentle, punning, says "love". For six years he has been building a canal boat on trestles in the back garden on Mill Lane, which "will never float". He changes from a man teased about it at every meal, working at night, to one who works in daylight, asks for help in public, finishes it and launches it on the Wenlow Arm in story 15. The boat's name, hidden under a sheet in story 13 and revealed in story 15, is Told You So.

### Jess Penhallow
Role: Ruth and Alan's daughter
29. Was an events coordinator in London. Small, dark, Alan's laugh, Ruth's eyebrows. Comes home in story 3 after losing her job and her relationship in the same week (the former partner is never named and never appears; call them only "her ex"). Starts on the bakery counter at Okafor's (story 7), moves to the early shift and learns to bake (stories 9-12), takes over the early bake, and moves into her own flat over the hardware shop on Sheep Street (story 14). Voice: at first apologetic and flat; by the end cheerful, blunt, up at four.

### Edna Trewin
Role: Ruth's mother
84. Lives at Larkhill House, a care home on the edge of Wenlow. Tiny, sharp, white hair set every Friday, cardigan buttoned wrong on purpose. Voice: Cornish, blunt, gives the short rude version of whatever Ruth would say in fifty minutes ("Men are idiots. Tell him to knock on the door."). Refuses every activity at the home ("I'm waiting, Ruth") until she dances seated with Clive Dunmore at the tea dance in story 14 and tells Ruth she is proud of her. Comes to the launch in story 15 with a hip flask.

### Samuel Okafor
Role: the baker downstairs; called Mr Okafor by everyone, including Ruth
63. Born in Lagos, came to Wenlow thirty-one years ago and opened Okafor's bakery. Broad, flour to the elbows, half-moon glasses, a white cap. ## Setting

Wenlow, a small fictional market town in the English Midlands, on a canal arm (the Wenlow Arm) with a slipway and a towpath. A market square, the High Street with the building society and the newsagent's, Sheep Street with Okafor's bakery and the hardware shop, the Corn Exchange (tango classes, the Wenlow Good Neighbour award evening), a cricket pavilion on the edge of town (Priya's wedding). Ruth's practice is two rooms up a steep staircase above Okafor's: a consulting room with two armchairs, a desk with a landline, a kettle, the fern on the windowsill, a sash window that sticks until story 13, old wiring that trips when the ovens and the kettle run together, and floorboards that warm at 10:15. The Penhallows live on Mill Lane, with a long back garden, a shed and the boat on trestles. Gordon and Desmond live side by side on Orchard Close. Marjorie lives on Bell Lane. Edna lives at Larkhill House, a care home with a lounge, a piano and an activities board. One year: stories 1-5 September and October, 6-10 November to January (story 8 just before Christmas), 11-15 April to June, ending on a Saturday in June.

Place names used in the book are only the ones above. The fern has no name. Walk-on characters not listed in Characters (the van driver, the care assistant, the newsagent, Hannah's sister, Dawn's family, Jess's ex) are never given names and are referred to only by what they are.

## Style

Comic fiction, warm and kind, English (British spelling and idiom). Close third person on Ruth, past tense, in every story. Each story is one session, about 3,000 words (between 2,700 and 3,400), a complete short story that can be read alone: its own comic situation, its turn and its ending, with enough context in the first page that a new reader needs nothing from earlier stories. Later stories may reward a reader who has read the earlier ones, but never depend on them.

The problems are slightly loony but ordinary, and every patient is treated kindly: the comedy comes from their situations and their earnestness, never from mocking them. Ruth is funny through understatement, not jokes. The moral of each story lands gently through what happens, never through a lecture, never as a closing paragraph that states the lesson; the moral lines in the Plot are for the writers, and should not appear in the text word for word. Ruth asks more than she tells. Common sense is the thread through the book, and it runs both ways: Ruth learns from her patients without anyone saying so.

Each story includes at least one beat of Ruth's own life or of the side characters, as the Plot and the table below give it, so the book is a portrait of a town and a family as well as five patients. Keep each side character's change in step with the table. The bakery downstairs (heat, smell, timers, the broom on the ceiling) is the practice's clock and recurs in every story, briefly.

Confidentiality is absolute and part of the comedy: Ruth never tells anyone about a patient, and when patients' lives touch hers (stories 11, 14 and 15) she keeps silent. Nothing clinical or heavy: no diagnoses, no medication, no crises. Dialogue is natural and sparing; patients' voices are distinct (see Characters). Use only the names in Characters and the places in Setting; do not invent new named people, clubs, teams or groups. English only.
